Summer is the best time to take a good look at your driveway. The weather is warm, the days are long, and whatever winter and spring did to the pavement is easy to spot. The heat isn't entirely friendly, though. Asphalt gets soft when it's hot, concrete expands and shifts, and one hard summer thunderstorm can show you every drainage flaw you didn't know you had.

Good summer driveway maintenance tasks aren't about making the driveway look great for a month. They're about catching small problems while they're still cheap and easy to handle, before water, traffic, and temperature swings turn them into big ones.

Why Summer Matters for Your Driveway

Your driveway puts up with a lot, such as tires, rain, sun, oil drips, dirt, yard debris, and a year's worth of freezing and thawing. By summer, the damage is out in the open. Dry, warm days are also good for certain jobs, like cleaning, filling cracks, and sealing asphalt. Always check the product label first, because each manufacturer has its own requirements.

Here's why timing matters. A thin crack in asphalt looks harmless in June. But if water gets in and freezes that winter, the ice expands and pushes the crack wider. Fixing it now is far easier than patching a pothole next spring.

1. Start by Walking the Whole Driveway

Before you sweep, scrub, or patch anything, walk the full length of the driveway and really look at it.

What to look for:

  • Long cracks, or a web of connected cracks in asphalt
  • Potholes and crumbling or loose pavement
  • Sunken or settled areas
  • Edges that are breaking apart
  • Puddles that stick around after rain
  • Damaged joints in concrete
  • Oil or chemical stains
  • Grass, soil, or mulch creeping onto the surface

Snap photos of anything that worries you. Looking at the same spot a year later is the easiest way to tell whether a crack is stable or getting worse.

Watch where the water goes: Check the driveway during or right after a rainstorm. Does water head toward your garage, your house, or a walkway? Does one low spot always hold a puddle? Those are worth taking seriously. And don't expect sealant to fix a drainage issue. If the slope is wrong or the ground underneath has settled, you have to deal with that cause directly.

2. Give It a Deep Clean

Dirt hides things, including cracks, stains, algae and moss, and worn patches. Clean first so you can see what you're dealing with. Sweep up leaves, gravel, and dust. If debris is packed into cracks or along the edges, a leaf blower helps before you sweep.

Tackling oil stains: Fresh spills are much easier than old ones that have soaked in. Cover the spot with an absorbent material to pull out what you can, then apply a degreaser made for driveways. Scrub with a stiff brush and rinse following the label directions. This step matters a lot if you plan to seal, because oil and grime keep sealer from sticking.

Go easy with the pressure washer: It's great for blasting out ground-in dirt and growth, but cranking up the power isn't always smart. The wrong nozzle, a wand held too close, or lingering in one spot can etch concrete and damage asphalt or an existing coating. Match your method and pressure to what your driveway is made of.

Keep degreaser and wash water out of storm drains. EPA's vehicle-washing guidance, written for municipal fleets, says not to send detergent wash water into a storm drain, and to clean leftover soap off the pavement so the next rain does not carry it there.

3. Fix Cracks Before They Grow

For asphalt driveways, filling cracks is one of the most worthwhile jobs on the list. Small cracks in solid pavement can usually be handled with a compatible crack filler. Bigger cracks, potholes, and widespread cracking are different, and they often mean something deeper is wrong.

When DIY works: Minor, isolated cracks are fine for a homeowner to tackle with a product made for your driveway material. Clean the crack, make sure it's dry, and follow the instructions on the package.

When to think bigger: Wide cracks, potholes, badly crumbling edges, and alligator cracking (the scaly, interconnected pattern) call for a closer look. If the base is weak, the drainage is poor, or the ground has settled, filling the visible damage is just a cosmetic bandage.

4. Think About Sealcoating Asphalt

Sealcoating can help an asphalt driveway that's in decent shape. It adds a protective layer against the weather and keeps the surface looking fresh. What it doesn't do is repair structural damage.

Pick your day carefully: Follow the manufacturer's rules for temperature, drying time, and rain. Warm, dry weather usually works best, but pavement that's scorching hot can cause application trouble with some products. Never seal a wet or dirty driveway.

Prep properly: Clean the surface, finish any crack repairs, and let everything dry fully before you start.

Apply it thin and even: Piling on a thick layer won't protect better, and it can cause curing and appearance problems.

Stay off it: Keep cars and foot traffic off for as long as the label says. Cool weather, humidity, or surprise rain can stretch that timeline.

5. Look After Concrete Joints

Concrete needs different care than asphalt. Expansion and control joints let the slabs move and help steer where cracks form. When the joint filler breaks down, weeds, dirt, and water get in.

Carefully pull out any loose or crumbling filler, clean the joint, and let it dry. Then, if it's appropriate for your driveway, install a compatible backer rod and joint sealant following the manufacturer's directions.

One caution: don't pack a joint with something rigid just because it looks tidier. The material has to flex with the slab's movement.

6. Keep Your Landscaping in Check

Your yard has more influence on your driveway than you might think. Overhanging branches drop leaves, sap, and seeds. Roots from big trees and shrubs can push pavement upward in some cases. Trim back vegetation, and keep soil from washing onto the surface during summer downpours.

Check the sprinklers: A head that keeps spraying one stretch of driveway leaves mineral deposits and keeps the pavement damp for no reason. Adjust your sprinklers so the water goes on the lawn and beds. And if your driveway slopes toward the house or garage, make sure your landscaping and irrigation aren't sending extra water that way.

7. Use Summer for Bigger Projects

Depending on your material and local climate, summer can also work well for larger jobs such as resurfacing, repairing edges, improving drainage, replacing failing sections, or upgrading the border. If the structure underneath is sound, you could also look at decorative options like stamped asphalt, resurfacing systems, or new edging. Handle structure and drainage first, and spend on looks second. A pretty surface over a failing base won't solve anything.

8. What Does Summer Driveway Maintenance Cost?

There isn't a single number, because the work ranges from a Saturday of sweeping and scrubbing to major pavement repair. Doing it yourself usually means inexpensive supplies like cleaners, crack filler, and brushes. Hiring a pro costs more, but you get specialized equipment, experienced labor, proper prep, and sometimes a warranty, depending on the contractor. Your final cost will depend on:

  • Driveway size
  • Surface material
  • How many cracks you have and how bad they are
  • Pothole repairs
  • How much cleaning is needed
  • Sealcoating or resurfacing
  • Drainage work
  • How easy the site is to access
  • Local labor and material prices

9. Your Summer Driveway Checklist

Run through this early or midway through summer:

  • Inspect the whole driveway for cracks and settling.
  • Take photos of anything you want to keep an eye on.
  • Sweep off dirt, leaves, and loose gravel.
  • Treat oil and other stains with the right products.
  • Clean the surface with care.
  • Fill minor cracks before they spread.
  • Deal with potholes and structural damage.
  • Check concrete expansion and control joints.
  • Watch how the driveway drains after heavy rain.
  • Trim plants and adjust sprinklers.
  • Seal asphalt only when the weather and product allow.
  • Book a professional for repairs that go beyond DIY.

Doing all this won't make your driveway immune to future damage, but it gives you the best chance of spotting trouble while it's still manageable.

When to Call a Professional

Plenty of driveway upkeep is homeowner-friendly. Cleaning, inspecting, and minor maintenance are doable if you have the right tools and follow the instructions. Bring in a pro when you notice widespread cracking, potholes that keep coming back, serious settling, major drainage trouble, or edges that are falling apart.

For example: If water still pools in the same place after you've cleaned and patched, the issue is probably the grading or the base beneath. That's a construction problem, not a cleaning one.

Final Thoughts

Regular maintenance is one of the simplest ways to learn how your driveway is aging. Summer driveway maintenance tasks like inspecting, cleaning, filling cracks, caring for joints, checking drainage, managing landscaping, and sealing at the right time all help you stay a step ahead of visible damage.
