There's no specific date circled on a calendar somewhere that tells you when to power wash your driveway. It depends on a handful of things, like what your driveway's made of, where you live, how much shade it gets, how many vehicles roll over it, and how fast dirt and grime seem to creep back after you've already cleaned it. Once a year is a flexible starting point for many homes, not a manufacturer requirement. If your driveway sits under a canopy of trees, sees daily traffic, deals with oil drips, or gets hit hard by pollen, mud, algae, or road salt in the winter, you'll probably need to clean it more often. On the flip side, a driveway that gets plenty of sun and doesn't see much use can often go longer between washes and still look fine. The point isn't to blast your driveway with a pressure washer as often as humanly possible. It's to clean it when the grime actually gets hard to remove and to use a method that won't chew up the surface in the process.

Why It Matters

Your driveway picks up a lot more than just dust over time. Tire marks, leaked fluids, tracked-in mud, pollen, algae, moss, and road salt. Concrete is especially prone to soaking up oil and other stains rather than letting them sit on the surface, which makes them harder to lift the longer you wait.

Cleaning regularly does more than make things look nicer. It also gives you a chance to notice cracks, drainage issues, or other wear before they turn into bigger problems. But here's the catch: too much pressure, or the wrong technique, can actually damage the driveway you're trying to protect. The goal is always a clean surface without unnecessary wear and tear.

Picture a concrete driveway shaded by a few big trees. Right after summer, it might look totally fine. But give it a few months of fallen leaves sitting in the damp, and you'll likely see dark staining creep in, staining that a sun-drenched driveway nearby might never develop at all.

Six Things That Change How Often You Should Clean

What It's Made Of

This is usually the first thing worth thinking about.

Concrete

It is porous, so it soaks up dirt, oil, and algae pretty readily. Cleaning once a year works for a lot of homeowners, though you may need to go more often if grime keeps coming back quickly.

Asphalt

It needs a gentler touch, too much pressure can actually tear up the surface or loosen material. Stick to a controlled, lower-pressure method built for asphalt specifically.

Pavers and Brick

This brings an extra wrinkle that is the joints. Water that's too strong, or aimed poorly, can knock the sand right out of them. So cleaning often needs to be followed up with joint maintenance or re-sanding.

Exposed Aggregate and Decorative Concrete

They have a texture that traps dirt in tiny crevices. A proper surface cleaner tends to give more even results than going at it with a narrow wand over and over.

Where You Live

Climate plays a bigger role than people expect. Humid regions tend to grow algae and mildew faster. Up north, road salt and winter grit are the main culprits. Dry climates deal more with dust and fine debris piling up. If you've just come out of a particularly wet season, or a winter with heavy salt use, it might be worth cleaning early, even if you're not technically "due" yet.

Sun vs. Shade

A driveway that stays damp for long stretches is going to need more attention than one that dries out quickly in direct sun. North-facing spots, areas under trees, or sections tucked beside a building tend to grow green or dark buildup faster than a sunny stretch of pavement. That's really why two houses right next to each other can end up on completely different cleaning schedules.

How Much It's Used

A driveway with one car parked occasionally isn't going to collect nearly as much grime as one that sees several vehicles every single day. Work trucks, multiple family cars, and any vehicle with a slow leak all speed up the buildup. Oil especially needs quick attention, concrete soaks it in, and the longer it sits, the harder it is to get out later. If you've got three cars in the driveway regularly, you're probably better off treating individual oil spots as they happen rather than waiting for the next full wash.

Seasonal Mess

Leaves, pollen, berries, mud, all of it can leave stains behind if it sits there too long. Sweeping regularly goes a long way toward keeping buildup manageable between deep cleans. After a big autumn leaf drop, a cleaning session is often worth it if organic debris has really packed in.

HOA Rules

If you're part of an HOA, it's worth checking their current guidelines on exterior upkeep. Some are stricter than others about driveway appearance. Your cleaning schedule should still be driven mainly by how the driveway actually looks and feels, but HOA expectations might push you to schedule cleanings a bit earlier than you otherwise would.

A Rough Schedule by Material

There's no one-size-fits-all answer here, but these are reasonable starting points:

Concrete

Once a year works for most homes. Heavy shade, lots of traffic, or noticeable organic growth might mean cleaning sooner. Spring tends to be a convenient time since it clears out winter grime and makes it easier to spot surface issues.

Asphalt

More pressure doesn't mean a better clean, it just means more risk of damage. A gentle annual wash, paired with spot-treating oil stains as they show up, usually does the trick.

Brick and Pavers

Annual cleaning is common, but keep an eye on the joints afterward. Check whether the sand's shifted and needs replacing. Sealing is its own separate decision, and it depends on the paver system and sealer you're using.

Stamped and Decorative Concrete

Clean gently to avoid wrecking the finish or sealer underneath. Once a year is usually fine, but reseal depends entirely on the sealer's current condition. Don't assume every decorative driveway needs the same treatment.

DIY or Call a Pro?

DIY pressure washing is doable if you've got the right equipment and know what you're doing. Pressure washers are genuinely capable of hurting you or damaging your driveway if you're not careful, this isn't just fine print.

Stick to the manufacturer's instructions, wear the right protective gear, keep kids and pets well clear of the work area, and never point that high-pressure spray at a person, an electrical outlet, a delicate surface, or, frankly, any part of your own body.

A professional typically brings surface cleaners and equipment matched to different driveway materials, which usually means better results without going over the same spot five times.

Signs You've Waited Too Long

You don't need a reminder on your phone, your driveway will tell you when it's time. Watch for:

  • Dark streaks or patches that stick around even after it rains
  • Green algae, moss, or mildew taking hold
  • A surface that gets slippery when wet
  • Oil or fluid stains that won't budge
  • Heavy grime built up along the tire paths
  • Organic debris that's worked its way into the surface itself
  • Grimy, discolored joints between pavers

If you clean it and discover cracks, crumbling edges, potholes, or real structural wear underneath, that's no longer a cleaning issue, that's a repair conversation.

Using Less Water and Fewer Chemicals

A spotless driveway doesn't have to mean soaking it in harsh chemicals. Regular sweeping alone cuts down the need for power washing. Treating stains while they're still fresh also reduces how aggressive your cleaning solution needs to be later.

A surface cleaner attachment tends to clean more evenly than repeatedly hammering one spot with a narrow spray. When you do use chemicals, pick something formulated for your driveway's material, follow the dilution and runoff guidance on the label. Use only the right amount of water, pressure, and cleaning product as the job genuinely calls for.

What Does It Cost?

Prices shift depending on where you live, how big the driveway is, what it's made of, how easy it is to access, and how stubborn the stains are. Angi's driveway pressure-washing guide, updated May 30, 2026, puts a typical project in a range of $100 to $500, with most people paying around $210. Treat that as a national planning figure, not a quote. Local price, size, material, access, and staining can land outside it.

Going the DIY route has its own costs too. Equipment rental, detergent, protective gear, and your own time all add up. If you're unsure, getting a few written quotes is the most reliable way to know what your driveway will actually cost to clean.

Final Thoughts

Start with an annual cleaning as a flexible baseline, then adjust based on your driveway's material, your local climate, how much shade it gets, how many vehicles use it, and how quickly grime tends to return. Concrete, asphalt, pavers, and decorative surfaces each deserve their own approach, treating them all the same is where a lot of driveways run into trouble.
