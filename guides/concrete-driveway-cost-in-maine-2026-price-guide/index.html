If you've started pricing out a new driveway, you've probably noticed the numbers aren't exactly consistent. One contractor quotes you one thing, another quotes you something else entirely, and you're left wondering who's right. The truth is, they're both probably right, concrete pricing depends on a handful of variables that shift from job to job, and Maine's climate adds a few wrinkles you won't find in a generic national pricing guide. A concrete driveway is still one of the smarter long-term investments a homeowner can make. It holds up well, looks clean for decades if it's installed correctly, and tends to outlast asphalt by a wide margin. But "installed correctly" matters a lot here, especially in a state where the ground freezes and thaws over and over each winter. Get the prep work wrong and you'll be patching cracks within a few years. Get it right and you're looking at a driveway that could outlast the mortgage.

So What Does It Actually Cost?

Most homeowners in Maine are paying somewhere between $8 and $18 per square foot for a concrete driveway in 2026. That's a wide range, and it's wide on purpose; a plain, no-frills slab sits at the low end, while anything with stamping, color, or extra reinforcement climbs toward the top. Rough numbers, by driveway size:

  • A single-car driveway (roughly 200 - 400 square feet) lands somewhere around $1,600 to $7,200
  • A two-car driveway (600 - 800 square feet) typically runs $4,800 to $14,400
  • Anything larger, or a driveway with custom features, can easily push past $15,000

Those figures cover materials and labor for a standard install. They don't necessarily cover extras like demolition of an old driveway, major grading, or drainage work which brings us to the part that actually drives the price up or down.

The Things That Move the Price Up (or Down)

Size and Shape

This is the obvious one. More square footage means more concrete, more labor hours, and a bigger bill.

Less obvious: a driveway with curves or an irregular footprint costs more to pour than a straight rectangle, even at the same square footage, because the formwork and finishing take more time.

How Thick the Slab Is

Standard concrete driveways run about 4 inches thick. In Maine, though, a lot of contractors will steer you toward 5 or 6 inches, since the extra thickness helps the slab handle the freeze-thaw cycles that wreak havoc on thinner pours. You'll pay more upfront for the added material, but it usually pays for itself by avoiding early cracking.

What's Happening Underneath

Before any concrete gets poured, the site needs to be prepped, old material removed, the ground graded and leveled, and a gravel base put down to handle drainage. If your lot is sloped, uneven, or has drainage issues, this step alone can add a noticeable amount to your total, simply because of the extra equipment time and labor involved.

Rebar and Wire Mesh

Reinforcement isn't strictly required everywhere, but in a climate like Maine's, it's hard to skip. Steel rebar or wire mesh inside the slab helps it resist the stress of expanding and contracting ground, which means fewer cracks down the road. It adds to the upfront cost, but it's one of those upgrades that quietly extends the life of the whole project.

Labor

Rates vary depending on where in Maine and who you hire, but skilled labor really matters here, more so than with a lot of home improvement projects. A crew that understands proper curing time, correct slope for drainage, and how to handle cold-weather pours will save you money in the long run, even if their quote looks a bit higher upfront.

Decorative Options

Stamped patterns, integral color, exposed aggregate, these all look great, but they're not cheap. Expect to add somewhere between $2 and $8 per square foot depending on how elaborate the finish is.

Where the Money Actually Goes

Roughly speaking, here's how your budget breaks down on a typical job:

  • Materials (concrete, gravel, rebar or mesh): 40 - 50%
  • Labor and installation: 40 - 50%
  • Finishing and sealing: about 10%

Knowing this breakdown is genuinely useful when you're trying to trim costs, it tells you where there's actual room to save versus where cutting corners will just cost you more later.

Why Maine's Climate Changes the Equation

Cold winters and repeated freeze-thaw cycles are tough on concrete. Without the right precautions, water gets into the slab, freezes, expands, and eventually cracks the surface. That's why proper drainage and expansion joints aren't optional extras here, they're part of doing the job right.

Most contractors working in Maine will also use air-entrained concrete, which has microscopic air pockets built into the mix. Those tiny pockets give freezing water somewhere to expand into, instead of pushing against the surrounding concrete and cracking it. It's a small detail that makes a big difference over a 20 or 30 year lifespan.

Concrete Compared to Other Driveway Materials

Concrete vs. Asphalt

Asphalt is cheaper to install, no argument there. But it needs resealing more often and doesn't last nearly as long as concrete in most cases.

Concrete vs. Gravel

Gravel is the budget option, full stop. It's also the least durable and the most maintenance-heavy over time, since it shifts and needs replenishing.

Concrete vs. Pavers

Pavers give you more design flexibility and a distinctive look, but you'll pay a premium for that flexibility. Concrete is the more affordable middle ground.

Realistic Ways to Bring the Cost Down

  • Skip the decorative finishes if budget is tight, a plain slab does the same job structurally
  • Time your project for the off-season, when contractors have more availability and sometimes more flexible pricing
  • Get quotes from at least three contractors before committing
  • Nail down your design and specs before the crew shows up, since mid-project changes get expensive fast

A little planning upfront tends to save real money. Most cost overruns on driveway projects come from changes made after the work has already started.

What Maintenance Actually Looks Like

Concrete driveways aren't maintenance-free, but they're close. The basics:

  • Reseal every 2 to 3 years
  • Clean off stains before they set in
  • Patch small cracks as soon as you notice them, before they spread

Stay on top of these, and a well-installed concrete driveway in Maine can realistically last 25 to 40 years. That kind of lifespan is hard to beat for the upfront cost.

Permits: Don't Skip This Step

Depending on your town or county, you may need a permit before you can pour a new driveway. Local rules often touch on drainage, where the driveway sits relative to your property line, and sometimes environmental factors if you're near wetlands or a water source. It's worth a quick call to your local permitting office, or just asking your contractor, most have dealt with this dozens of times and know exactly what's required.

Picking the Right Contractor

This is where a lot of projects succeed or fail. Look for someone with real, demonstrable experience pouring driveways specifically in Maine, not just concrete work in general. Check their reviews, ask for references, and be wary of any quote that seems vague or unusually low. A contractor worth hiring will walk you through material choices, talk you through the timeline, and give you a written estimate that actually breaks down where your money is going.

Final Thoughts

A concrete driveway is a real investment, and in Maine specifically, it's one where the details like thickness, reinforcement, drainage matter more than they might in a milder climate. Spend the time upfront to understand what you're paying for, and you'll end up with a driveway that holds up for decades instead of years.
