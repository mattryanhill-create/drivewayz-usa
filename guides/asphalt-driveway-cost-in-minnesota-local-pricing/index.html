Getting a few paving quotes is the initial part of planning an asphalt driveway in Minnesota. What you end up paying depends on the soil, the drainage, how much digging is involved, and what the weather does to the ground every year. If you understand those pieces early, your budget gets more realistic and you're less likely to pay for fresh asphalt that sits on top of an unsolved problem.

How Much Does an Asphalt Driveway Cost in Minnesota?

Two things drive the total: how much pavement you need and how much work the site needs before any asphalt goes down. Paving over a solid, well-prepared surface can cost far less than tearing out an old driveway, fixing weak soil, and sorting out drainage. According to Angi's March 2026 Minneapolis cost data, asphalt driveways average $2.90 to $4.50 per square foot. Treat that as a planning figure, not a quote. To give you a starting point, here are common driveway sizes to measure against:

These are rough figures. They likely leave out demolition, heavy excavation, drainage work, and permits. Prices also shift between the Twin Cities and smaller towns, so expect your own numbers to differ.

What Affects Asphalt Driveway Pricing in Minnesota?

Even driveways of the same dimensions can come in at very different prices. The condition of the ground often counts for as much as the amount of asphalt.

Size and Layout

More square footage means more material and more labor. Add a wider pad, an extra parking spot, or a long approach, and the price climbs. Shape matters too. A plain rectangle is easy to pave. A curved driveway that squeezes between flower beds and a garage, or one with tight access and awkward slopes, takes more time and care.

Excavation and Removing the Old Surface

For a brand-new driveway, the crew may have to clear vegetation, dig out soil, and shape the ground for a compacted gravel base. If you're replacing an old driveway, the cracked asphalt or concrete has to be broken up and hauled off. That means equipment, disposal fees, and extra labor. When you review an estimate, check whether removal and disposal are included or billed on a separate line.

Aggregate Base

The layer under the asphalt does more work than most people realize. It supports the pavement and spreads the weight of vehicles across the soil below. In Minnesota's climate, this layer is especially important. A weak base or unstable ground underneath can lead to sinking, cracks, and bumpy spots. How thick the base should be, and what it's made of, depends on the soil, the drainage, the loads it will carry, and the design. A good contractor looks at your property first rather than using one spec for every job.

Why Minnesota Weather Matters

Winters here are cold and snowy, and temperatures swing back and forth across freezing again and again. That's hard on pavement, particularly when water gets into cracks or pools underneath.

Frozen moisture pushes on the soil and the pavement around it. When the ground thaws, any poorly supported area can sink or form dips. So drainage and base work belong at the center of an asphalt project, not on the optional extras list.

The Minnesota Department of Natural Resources says permeable pavement needs a properly designed aggregate reservoir to handle stormwater and avoid freeze-thaw damage. That's a different type of pavement, but the lesson carries over. The foundation has to be designed for local conditions.

New Driveway or Resurfacing?

You don't always need to rip everything out. The right call depends on how the existing pavement and the base below it are holding up.

A New Asphalt Driveway

A new driveway typically involves clearing and grading the site, laying and compacting an aggregate base, and then placing the asphalt. If the soil is soft, the ground is uneven, or water isn't draining well, there will be additional work. That prep raises the upfront cost, but it creates the foundation the pavement relies on.

Resurfacing

Resurfacing puts a fresh layer over existing asphalt when it's still in good enough shape. It can refresh the look of a driveway without a full rebuild. It won't fix everything, though. Widespread cracking, serious settling, failed drainage, or a worn-out base usually call for deeper repairs or replacement. Have a contractor inspect the driveway before agreeing to an overlay.

How Drainage Changes the Price

Where water goes matters a great deal. Rain or snowmelt that collects by the garage, runs across the surface, or seeps into the pavement will wear the driveway down over time. Depending on your property, the fix might be reshaping the slope, redirecting a downspout, adding a drain, or adjusting the grade around the driveway.

Example: Picture a driveway that gets potholes near the street every spring. If someone simply repaves that spot without finding out where the water is coming from and how the base is holding up, the same potholes will probably come back. Dealing with the cause can raise the estimate, but it often pays off.

When to Install an Asphalt Driveway in Minnesota

Asphalt needs the right conditions. The ground should be prepared, and the surface shouldn't be wet or frozen when paving starts. MnDOT's Standard Specifications for Construction (2025 edition, Section 2360) also bar placing asphalt on road projects when weather, roadbed, or surface moisture conditions are unfavorable. The exact rules for a home driveway depend on the material and the method used.

Rather than picking a date because it's convenient, talk it through with a qualified contractor. Paving in suitable conditions gives the crew a better chance to place and compact the material properly.

Getting an Accurate Estimate

Start by measuring your driveway roughly and noting any visible trouble: potholes, standing water, crumbling edges, and spots that have sunk. Then ask each contractor to walk you through what they plan to do, including:

  • Excavation and removal of old pavement
  • Base material and compaction
  • Asphalt thickness and how it will be installed
  • Grading and drainage needs
  • Cleanup, disposal, and any permits
  • Workmanship warranty and maintenance advice

Compare the full scope of each bid, not just the price per square foot.

Maintenance After Installation

A little regular care helps you catch small problems early. Keep drainage paths open, clear off debris, and look over the pavement after winter. Fix cracks and potholes based on how serious they are, and ask your contractor when sealcoating makes sense for your particular driveway.

New asphalt needs time to cure and settle, so follow your contractor's instructions. Don't assume it should be sealed right away. If the same spot keeps cracking or sinking, look at drainage and the base rather than patching the surface again and again.

Final Thoughts

Knowing local asphalt driveway costs in Minnesota helps you set a realistic budget, but the cheapest quote isn't always the best value over the years. Size, soil, weather, drainage, and the quality of the work all shape how long the pavement lasts.
