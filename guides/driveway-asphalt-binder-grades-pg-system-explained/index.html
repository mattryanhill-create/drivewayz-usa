When homeowners plan a new driveway, they usually think about thickness, color, and maybe how long it'll last before it needs sealing. What most homeowners don't think about is the asphalt binder. That binder, and the way it's graded, has more to do with how your driveway holds up than almost anything else in the mix. This is where the PG system comes in. PG stands for Performance Grade. It's a way of classifying asphalt binders based on the temperatures and stresses they're built to handle. The system came out of the Superpave research program, and today it's the standard most transportation agencies rely on when they write asphalt specifications. You don't need an engineering degree to use this information. If you understand roughly what a PG grade tells you, you'll be in a much better position to evaluate what a contractor is proposing and to ask sharper questions before any asphalt gets laid down.

What Asphalt Binder Does

Asphalt binder is that dark, tar-like petroleum product that glues aggregate. The crushed stone and sand into a solid mat. The aggregate does most of the heavy lifting structurally, but the binder is what holds everything together and keeps it from falling apart.

Picture your driveway right after it's poured. All you see is a smooth black surface. Underneath, though, is an engineered blend of stone and binder, and how well that blend was designed determines how the driveway reacts to cars rolling over it every day and to temperatures swinging from summer to winter.

A good binder needs to do two opposite things, stay firm enough in the heat that it doesn't deform, and stay flexible enough in the cold that it doesn't crack. Get the mix wrong for your climate, and you'll start seeing ruts, cracks, or general breakdown well before you should.

Breaking Down the PG Numbers

The PG system labels each binder with two numbers. Take PG 64 -22 as an example:

  • The 64 is the high-temperature grade.
  • The -22 is the low-temperature grade.

Both numbers are in Celsius, and they correspond to expected pavement temperatures, not air temperatures. So a PG 64-22 binder is engineered to hold up around 64°C on the hot end and -22°C on the cold end.

Remember these numbers aren't guarantees. They don't mean your driveway will never crack below -22°C or never rut above 64°C. Real-world performance is shaped by the whole asphalt mixture, the pavement structure underneath, traffic patterns, how well the job was built, and the environment it sits in, not just the binder grade alone.

Pavement Temperature vs. Air Temperature: Why It Matters

Ask anyone who's walked barefoot across a driveway in July. Pavement gets a lot hotter than the air around it once the sun hits it directly. That's exactly why engineers don't just look at a city's average weather when picking a binder.

Instead, PG selection leans on actual pavement temperature data. The Federal Highway Administration's LTPPBind software, for instance, pulls in regional climate information to help figure out the right high- and low-temperature grades for a specific site.

For driveways, this distinction matters more than people expect. Two houses in different parts of the country, even in the same general region, can see very different pavement conditions depending on sun exposure, elevation, and local climate patterns. A house up north might need a binder that shrugs off deep cold snaps, while one in the Deep South might need something built to resist softening under blazing summer heat.

Grades You'll Commonly See on Driveway Jobs

There's no universal "correct" PG grade for residential driveways. Specs commonly mention grades like PG 58-28, PG 64-22, PG 70-22, and PG 76-22, but which one actually fits your project comes down to local climate, expected traffic, how the pavement is designed, and whatever your state or municipality requires.

PG 64-22, handles up to roughly 64°C on the high end and down to about -22°C on the low end. Bumping up the high-temperature number makes sense where rutting is a real concern, heavier loads, hotter pavement.

Don't fall into the trap of assuming a bigger number automatically means a tougher, better driveway. The right binder is the one suited to your actual conditions, not the one with the most impressive-looking on sheet. The Asphalt Institute keeps state-by-state data on PG and PG-Plus specifications, and it's worth noting how much those requirements shift from one state to the next.

Climate and Traffic: The Two Big Variables

Climate drives a lot of the decision-making around binder grade, but it's not the whole story.

Heat and Rutting

When temperatures climb, asphalt softens. Add repeated vehicle weight on top of that softened surface, and you see rutting, permanent grooves or depressions. This shows up more often on driveways that regularly deal with heavier vehicles like pickups, RVs, trailers, or a lot of tight turning.

Cold and Cracking

Go the other direction, and cold makes binder stiffer and more brittle. If the pavement can't flex enough to handle contraction as temperatures drop, thermal cracks start forming. The low-temperature side of the PG grade exists specifically to head off this problem. The FHWA ties low-temperature binder requirements directly to reducing that cracking risk.

Traffic Loading

A typical two-car driveway isn't under anywhere near the stress of a highway. But if your household regularly parks a heavy truck, tows a trailer, or sees a lot of delivery and commercial traffic, that's a factor worth raising with your contractor and it's something FHWA's selection tools account for too, alongside vehicle speed.

Is a Higher PG Grade Automatically Better?

No, and this might be the single most useful thing to take away from all of this.

A higher number isn't a shortcut to a better driveway. The whole point of the PG system is matching the binder's properties to the environment and loading it'll actually face. Installing an unnecessarily high-temperature binder in a mild climate probably won't do much for a lightly used residential driveway. On the flip side, skimping on low-temperature performance in a cold-winter region is asking for cracking problems. The goal isn't the highest grade you can get. It's the right grade for where you live and how you'll use the driveway.

A Word on Polymer-Modified Binders

Polymer modification changes how a binder behaves, often improving its resistance to specific kinds of damage. These modified binders show up in projects facing tougher conditions: extreme heat, heavy traffic, unusually demanding loads.

Be skeptical, though, of sales pitches claiming a polymer-modified binder will add some specific number of years to your driveway's life or cut cracking by an exact percentage. Those outcomes depend on the entire pavement system, not the binder in isolation. The Asphalt Institute notes that some agencies layer on additional PG-Plus requirements for modified binders, including specs tied to AASHTO M332.

The Binder Is Only One Part of the Equation

Even a perfectly chosen PG grade can't rescue a driveway built on a poor foundation. Solid results depend on a handful of things working together:

  • Proper subgrade preparation
  • Adequate drainage
  • The right aggregate structure
  • Sound mixture design
  • Careful placement
  • Thorough compaction
  • Appropriate thickness for the load
  • Generally sound construction practices

Compaction in particular deserves attention. FHWA points to it as one of the biggest factors in how flexible pavement performs over time. Since it drives down air voids and boosts density, stiffness, and overall durability. If a contractor spends the whole conversation talking about binder grade and never mentions drainage, base prep, or compaction, you're only getting part of the picture.

Questions Worth Asking Your Contractor

Before you sign off on a driveway project, it's worth pinning your contractor down on specifics:

  • What PG binder grade are you specifying for my property?
  • Is that grade based on the local climate and expected pavement conditions here?
  • What asphalt mixture are you planning to use?
  • How will you prepare the base and subgrade?
  • What's your compaction process?
  • Can the binder or mixture be documented by your supplier?

Final Thoughts

A driveway that actually lasts starts with materials chosen for the conditions they'll really face. The PG system gives you a practical framework for understanding how asphalt binder is classified for temperature performance, but it's only one piece of a much bigger puzzle.
