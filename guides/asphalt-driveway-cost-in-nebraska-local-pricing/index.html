Pricing a new asphalt driveway in Nebraska takes more than measuring the area and calling a paver. What's under the surface often drives the bill: how much digging is needed, what the soil is like, how the base is built, where the water goes, and what local crews charge. Knowing these pieces up front makes it much easier to set a realistic budget and avoid nasty surprises.

Costs swing quite a bit from one property to the next. Paving a driveway over a solid, well-prepared base is a very different job from tearing out a crumbling one that has drainage or base problems.

Weather matters here too. Nebraska's freeze-thaw swings, spring moisture, and winter snow all take a toll on pavement, so how the driveway is built and cared for has a real effect on how long it lasts.

What Does an Asphalt Driveway Cost in Nebraska?

There's no single statewide price. Online calculators start from different assumptions, and many leave out demolition, so treat any figure you find as a ballpark, not a quote. Real bids can land above or below it depending on your site. Size is the biggest lever. A short, single-car driveway is a small job. A long rural drive means far more material, more machine time, and more prep.

To get a number you can trust, measure the length and width, take note of the driveway's current condition, and ask a local paving contractor for a detailed written quote.

What Drives the Price?

Size and Shape

Square footage is the starting point. Larger area means more asphalt and more labor, though the price per square foot doesn't always scale evenly, since crews still have to cover equipment hauling and setup no matter how big the job is.

Shape matters, too. A plain rectangle is quick to lay out and pave. Curves, a second parking pad, tight spots, or a turnaround add time.

If you're planning a two-car driveway, think about how you'll really use it. Do you need room to open car doors, turn around, or fit a guest's vehicle now and then? Settling on dimensions before the work starts is far cheaper than changing them afterward.

Excavation and Base Work

What sits under the asphalt matters as much as the asphalt. Crews may have to strip topsoil, haul out bad fill, or remove old pavement before they can put in a proper gravel base.

If the soil is soft or drains poorly, expect extra digging, stabilization, or more base stone. That pushes the upfront cost up, but cutting corners here is how driveways end up sinking, cracking, and developing potholes. A well-compacted base is what carries the daily weight of your vehicles.

Thickness and Materials

How thick the asphalt needs to be depends on what's driving over it, what's underneath, and how the pavement is designed. A driveway for family cars isn't built the same way as one that sees heavy trucks or equipment.

A good contractor will spell out the thickness and base specs in the estimate. If they don't, you can't really compare bids, because two quotes with the same total may be offering very different driveways.

Labor, Equipment, and Access

Local wage rates, asphalt supply, how far equipment and material must travel, and how easy it is to get machines onto your property all play a part.

Tight access can mean extra equipment moves or a different paving method. And a long country driveway can be pricey even if the surface is simple, because of the sheer amount of material and prep involved.

How Nebraska Weather Affects Asphalt

Nebraska's temperature swings are hard on pavement. Water seeps into cracks, freezes, expands, and widens the damage. Repeat that cycle every winter and small cracks turn into big problems, especially if water gets trapped under the surface.

The Nebraska Department of Transportation explains that potholes form when water seeps through pavement cracks into the material below and goes through repeated freeze-thaw cycles. That's why drainage and quick crack repair matter so much for homeowners here.

During installation, the contractor should grade the driveway so rain and snowmelt run off instead of pooling in low spots. The subgrade and base should also be built to suit your soil and moisture conditions. Timing counts as well. Paving at a sensible time of year and letting the asphalt cool and cure the way your contractor advises will help the new surface hold up.

New Installation or Resurfacing?

Not every driveway needs to be ripped out. It comes down to the shape the pavement and its base are in.

Starting from Scratch

A brand-new driveway can involve clearing the site, excavating, grading, laying and compacting a stone base, and then paving. If you need drainage features or extra soil stabilization, those should be written into the scope of work. The prep often takes up a big chunk of the budget, but it's what the whole driveway rests on.

Resurfacing What You Have

Resurfacing, also called an overlay, means putting a fresh layer of asphalt on top of existing pavement. It can work well when the surface is worn but the driveway is still structurally sound.

It's usually the wrong move if you've got widespread cracking, sunken areas, poor drainage, or a failing base. Have a contractor look at the pavement before anyone recommends an overlay, because a new layer over a structural problem just hides it for a while.

Costs People Forget to Budget For

The paving line item may not cover everything. Ask whether your estimate includes:

  • Removal and disposal of the old pavement
  • Excavation and removal of bad soil
  • Base stone and compaction
  • Drainage fixes and regrading
  • Curb or apron work
  • Edging and tie-ins to existing surfaces
  • Cleanup and restoring the site

Keeping Your Driveway in Good Shape

Walk the driveway now and then and look for cracks, puddles, crumbling edges, and small dips. Deal with drainage issues quickly, keep water paths clear, and get repairs done before damage spreads. Sealcoating can protect suitable asphalt, but it won't fix structural problems. Follow your contractor's advice on when to do the first coat and how often to repeat it.

In winter, be gentle with plows and shovels so you don't gouge the surface, and try not to head into freezing weather with open cracks or drainage problems still unaddressed.

Final Thoughts

Getting a handle on asphalt driveway costs in Nebraska starts with looking hard at your property, how you'll use the driveway, and what has to happen underneath. Size, soil, asphalt specs, drainage, and local labor all feed into the final number.
