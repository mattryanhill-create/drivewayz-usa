Picking a driveway contractor isn't just about who gave you the lowest bid or which material looks best in front of your house. Whether you're planning an asphalt driveway, a poured concrete slab, or a repair job on an existing surface, one detail can save you a massive headache later is the contractor's insurance. You need to know what kind of insurance contractors carry, whether it's still active, and whether it even applies to the work they're about to do on your property. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners says, ask for proof of liability and workers' comp coverage, then call the insurer directly to confirm it's real.

Why Driveway Contractor Insurance Matters

Driveway work isn't gentle. There's heavy equipment rolling around, trucks backing in and out, excavation happening a few feet from your foundation, and crews working close to your house and your neighbor's fence line. Even a modest driveway job carries real risk of property damage or someone getting hurt.

Example:

Picture a crew tearing out an old concrete driveway, and mid-excavation, a piece of machinery cracks your foundation or slices through a buried utility line. If that contractor is properly covered, the claim can typically move through their liability policy, subject to whatever terms and exclusions apply. If they're not covered? You could be the one stuck sorting out the mess.

Checking insurance also tells you something about who you're hiring. A contractor who can produce clean, current documentation without hesitation is usually a very different operation than one who's winging it project to project.

What Coverage Should You Ask?

There's no one-size-fits-all insurance package every contractor carries. It shifts based on state law, whether they have employees, what vehicles they run, and what the project itself involves. That said, a few categories are worth asking about every time.

General Liability Insurance

This is the big one. General liability is what typically responds to third-party claims. Like property damage or bodily injury tied to the contractor's work. If a crew accidentally backs a skid steer into your neighbor's mailbox or fence, this is the policy that (depending on the circumstances and the fine print) may cover it.

Don't just assume a liability policy covers everything a contractor does. Ask directly whether it extends to the specific work you need, like asphalt paving, concrete pours, excavation, grading, sealcoating, whatever's on your project list.

And skip the myth that every job needs a flat $1 million in coverage. Some contractors carry that much because a city contract or a client demanded it, not because it's some universal residential requirement. What's "enough" really depends on your specific project and any requirements attached to it.

Workers' Compensation Insurance

Workers' comp exists to cover employees who get hurt or sick because of the job. But whether it's actually required depends entirely on your state. Some states carve out exemptions based on how many people a company employs, how the business is structured, or the type of work being done.

If there's a crew showing up to work on your property, ask two things: does the company carry workers' comp at all, and are the specific people on your job actually covered under it? A verbal "yeah, we're covered" isn't proof of anything, ask to see current documentation and, when it matters, verify it.

Commercial Auto Insurance

Most driveway crews run pickup trucks, dump trucks, and trailers to haul equipment and materials to your property. Here's a common misconception:

A personal auto insurance usually isn't built to cover a vehicle being used for business purposes the way a commercial policy is. If a contractor's truck clips your car or damages your property while making a delivery, you'll want to know their vehicles are actually covered for business use, especially on bigger jobs with multiple truck trips.

Equipment Coverage

Paving and installing a driveway typically calls for compactors, rollers, skid steers, excavators, and real machinery. Many contractors carry equipment or inland marine coverage that protects that gear while it's being hauled or used off their home base.

This type of coverage is mostly there to protect the contractor's own tools, not your property directly. But it's still a decent signal that you're dealing with a business that's thought through the risks of the trade, rather than someone operating on a shoestring.

How to Verify This

Asking for proof is step one. Reading it carefully is step two and it's the step a lot of homeowners skip.

Get a Current Certificate of Insurance

A COI is a summary document. It lists the insurer, policy numbers, coverage types, and effective dates. Useful, but it's not the actual policy, and it's not a guarantee. If the project is a significant one, or something feels off, call the agent or carrier yourself using contact information you looked up independently, not a number the contractor handed you.

Check the Dates

An insurance certificate that expired three months ago tells you nothing about today. Your project needs to happen while the policy is actually in force. If the document looks stale, ask for a fresh one.

Match the Business Name

The name on your estimate should line up with the name on the contract, the insurance paperwork, and any licensing documents. If the quote comes from "ABC Driveways" but the insurance certificate says "XYZ Paving LLC," that's worth a direct conversation before you sign anything. There might be a reasonable explanation like a DBA, a parent company, a subcontracting setup but you want to hear it, not guess.

Ask Who's Actually Doing the Work

Plenty of driveway contractors bring in subcontractors for excavation, grading, drainage, or concrete. Find out who's really showing up to your property and whether they carry their own coverage. For a larger project, it's reasonable to ask for proof from subcontractors too, and some contracts will spell out whether you need to be added as an additional insured.

Red Flags Worth Slowing Down For

Be a little more cautious if a contractor:

  • Won't produce proof of insurance, period.
  • Hands you a certificate that's expired or missing key details.
  • Can't tell you who their insurer or agent even is.
  • Has coverage that doesn't seem to match the work they're proposing.
  • Gives you a business name on the quote that doesn't match the insurance paperwork.
  • Deflects when you bring up workers' comp.
  • Pushes hard for a big upfront payment before you've seen basic documentation.

None of these on their own prove someone's dishonesty. But they're all good reasons to pause, ask more questions, and confirm what you're actually being offered before crews show up.

Insurance and Bonding: Are Not the Same

This one trips people up constantly. Insurance covers losses under a policy. A bond is something different, it's tied to obligations under a contract.

A performance bond, for example, gives you some assurance the contracted work will actually get finished. A payment bond helps protect against unpaid subcontractors or suppliers coming after you later. The U.S. Small Business Administration describes both as forms of contract surety bonds, commonly required on public projects and sometimes on private ones too. Whether your driveway job needs a bond really comes down to the contract and any local requirements. It's not something every residential project automatically needs.

Will My Homeowners Insurance Cover Problems Instead?

No, and you shouldn't count on it that way. Your homeowners policy and the contractor's insurance are built for different jobs. Yours protects your home and belongings, subject to its own deductibles, limits, and exclusions, it was never meant to stand in for a contractor's own coverage. Confirm the contractor is properly insured before the first shovel hits the ground, rather than hoping your own policy will bail you out if something goes sideways.

Quick Checklist Before You Hire a Contractor

  • Is the contractor currently insured, and can they prove it?
  • Does their liability coverage actually match the work you're having done?
  • Is workers' comp applicable, and is it in force?
  • Are their vehicles insured for commercial use?
  • Will subcontractors be involved, and are they covered too?
  • Can you independently verify coverage with the agent or carrier?
  • Do the business names match across every document?
  • Does the project require any bonding?
  • Does the written contract clearly spell out the scope of work, payment terms, and warranty?

Final Thoughts

A new driveway is a real investment, and checking insurance should be a normal part of picking who does the work, not an afterthought. You don't need to become an insurance expert to do this well. You just need documentation that's current, legitimate, and actually relevant to the job being done.

Treat it the same way you'd treat checking permits, licenses, references, and the contract itself. A few extra minutes upfront can spare you a much bigger problem down the road.
